Two codings warrant specific note. Records **104 and 105** — the Espinoza pair — lose their panel coding in the reduced extraction. The abstracts state that a weighted scoring methodology is used and that criteria derive "from both literature and practice", and record 105's limitation note says the *evaluator* must possess the knowledge to assign weights. None of this identifies who did so; the cross-functional internal team is established only from the full texts. Under rule 3 both are `unclear` here, while the full extractions retain the panel finding on full-text evidence.

Record **117** is the clearest illustration of why rule 3 matters. Its abstract states plainly that the Best–Worst Method is employed for weighting — the method is named, but the source of the best-worst judgments is not. Coding this `author` would have been a guess about the one record in the corpus whose method most closely matches this thesis's own.

## What this means for Chapter 2

**The claim changes shape, and becomes both narrower and harder to dispute.**

The v1 phrasing — *"22 records assign weights by author judgment; exactly one reports a panel with a stated size"* — rested on inference in both halves. Neither figure survives.

The v2 claim, stated at abstract level and fully checkable:

> Of the 143 studies included, 54 apply a procedure requiring criteria weights. **None states in its abstract how many experts contributed to those weights.** Six report that a group of experts or decision-makers was involved, without giving a number. Forty-seven — 87% of the records that weight anything — do not disclose the source of their weights at all.

Two consequences follow. First, the **empirical-elicitation gap holds**: no work in the corpus reports eliciting weights from a specified panel of practitioners, still less from practitioners across organisations, and none reports an inter-rater agreement measure. Second, and independently, the corpus exhibits a **reporting-practice problem** worth a paragraph of its own in §2.5.2 — a literature in which seven of eight weighted studies leave the provenance of their weights undisclosed is a literature whose weight-based conclusions cannot be assessed by a reader.

That second observation strengthens the methodological case for this thesis considerably: it is not only that no one has elicited weights from practitioners, but that the field's reporting standard makes it impossible to tell what most authors did. Pre-registering the elicitation protocol and publishing the anonymised judgments addresses a deficiency the corpus demonstrates at scale.

**Where the full-text findings go.** The five works read in full retain their full-text codings in their own extraction files. In §2.6, where those works are discussed individually, the panel details are reported with full texts as the source — Zhou & Chen's three internal directors, Espinoza's cross-functional team, Tlemcani's deferred Delphi proposal. Corpus-level tallies and work-level discussion draw on different evidence bases, and the text should say so once, plainly, rather than blurring them.
