# Recode v2 — fields: AIRD and SECC, records 1–143

**Why these fields are being recoded.** The v2 criteria test — *code `yes` only where the criterion is a dimension on which something is evaluated, compared, measured or selected* — was written as a universal rule for all six criteria, but applied in the first recoding pass only to TCO, PORT and TTDP. AIRD and SECC were left on their v1 codings because they had passed the first validation. This produced an internal inconsistency: three criteria on the strict test, three on the loose one. The second validation round surfaced it, with AIRD falling from κ = 1.00 to 0.30 and SECC from 0.80 to 0.38 — not because coders disagreed about the abstracts, but because they were applying different definitions.

This pass puts all six criteria on the same test.

## What changed and why

**SECC falls from 53% of the corpus to 31%.** The v1 coding marked `yes` wherever security, governance, privacy or compliance was mentioned as a concern. Under the strict test, a paper that *proposes a governed architecture* is coded `yes` — governance is what it delivers and what it should be judged on — but a paper that merely notes governance among the challenges motivating its work is `no`. Roughly thirty records fell on that distinction, mostly mesh and lakehouse papers whose abstracts cite governance as context for a contribution about something else.

This resolves the discriminance concern flagged earlier. A criterion present in 53% of a corpus and concentrated almost entirely in one of its two halves would have separated configurations weakly. At 31%, distributed across both the domain and methods groups, SECC behaves like a criterion rather than like background vocabulary.

**AIRD falls from 22% to 8%,** and the reason is specific. The dominant v1 pattern was coding `yes` for any paper mentioning AI or analytics as its application context — "AI-ready pipelines for healthcare", "AI is being used in public sector organizations", "enterprise AI platforms rely on lakehouse architectures". In each case AI is the *reason the work exists*, not a dimension on which anything is evaluated. Under the strict test these become `no`.

The twelve surviving records are those where AI or ML capability is genuinely assessed: architectures compared on their support for ML workloads (32, 68, 94, 104, 105), AI platforms as the alternatives being ranked (22, 124), AI-readiness measured as an outcome (46), analytics capability as an evaluated property (20, 70, 111), and semantic fidelity in ML pipelines as a measured consequence (23).

## Implications

**The criteria coverage map, final form:**

| Criterion | Records | Share |
|---|---|---|
| TCO | 52 | 36% |
| SECC | 44 | 31% |
| PORT | 26 | 18% |
| AIRD | 12 | 8% |
| CARB | 8 | 6% |
| TTDP | 6 | 4% |

**This is a materially different picture from v1, and a more defensible one.** Under the loose test the map showed a gradient from 53% down to 4%, with the shape driven partly by how often words appear in abstracts. Under the strict test it shows something sharper: **cost and governance are evaluated dimensions in roughly a third of the corpus; everything else is evaluated in under a fifth, and three of the six criteria in under a tenth.**

The claim available for Chapter 2 is now precise on all six criteria at once. Of 143 studies concerned with selecting or comparing data architectures and platforms:

- **Delivery time** is an evaluation dimension in 6 (4%), and none measures elapsed time to a production data product.
- **Carbon** in 8 (6%), and none normalises emissions to a functional unit or uses the result to choose between architectures.
- **AI readiness** in 12 (8%), and none addresses vector search, RAG or agent access as evaluated capabilities.
- **Portability** in 26 (18%).
- **Governance and compliance** in 44 (31%).
- **Cost** in 52 (36%).

The three criteria this thesis treats as its research depth — carbon, delivery time, AI readiness — are precisely the three least evaluated in the literature, each appearing in under a tenth of the corpus. That alignment is worth stating explicitly in §2.7: the thesis is not adding arbitrary criteria, it is adding the three that the field discusses and does not measure.

**Methodological note for §2.2.6.** The AIRD and SECC recoding was triggered by the second validation round, which showed both fields falling from high agreement to low. The cause was not coder disagreement about abstracts but a definitional inconsistency introduced when the strict criteria test was applied to three of six criteria. Recording this in the protocol — a rule revised, applied incompletely, the omission detected by validation, then corrected — demonstrates that the validation procedure does what it is there for. A validation round that never changes anything is not evidence of quality.
